## Fifty minutes of grounding

If you read nothing else, the literacy pathway's plain-language modules cover the mechanism, the craft, and the policy:

1. [How AI Works](pathway/how-ai-works.md): why confident output is not always correct output, and why that is the mechanism rather than a glitch.
2. [Prompting Fundamentals](pathway/prompting.md): the habits that most improve results, starting with giving the model your actual materials.
3. [The Policy in Practice](pathway/rules.md): what the policy asks when AI contributes to your work, including which information stays out of public tools.
4. [Teaching and Assessment](pathway/teaching-assessment.md): where AI genuinely helps in course preparation and evaluation, and where it needs your expert review.

## Patient data, student records, and licensed content: what the policy asks {: #the-lines-that-never-move }

The full detail lives in [The Policy in Practice](pathway/rules.md) and the [AI Responsible Use Policy](governance/policy.md); the short version:

Public tools may keep, learn from, or pass on what you type, so the policy names four kinds of information that stay out of them unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has vetted and approved a tool for that data: protected health information (PHI); student records covered by the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA), such as grades, evaluations, and identifiable student work; faculty and staff confidential personnel information; and AUA proprietary business information, unpublished research data, or trade secrets. The list is not exhaustive, and in practice it includes research participant data. Your own unpublished drafts are yours to work on in a paid plan with training on your content turned off, as [AI for Research](tools/research.md) explains.

Licensed question-bank content is third-party material, and the policy asks for a review before it goes into an AI tool, because its license may not allow that.

Whatever a tool contributed, the work you sign is yours. Read AI-drafted teaching material as its content expert before students see it: models make confident errors, and students are the readers least placed to catch them.
