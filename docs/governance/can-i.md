# Can I Use AI for This?

<span class="meta-chip meta-status">Draft for AI Committee comment</span> <span class="meta-chip">For everyone</span>

Short answers to common questions about using artificial intelligence (AI) at the American University of Antigua (AUA), each with its conditions and a link to the detail. This is a plain-language guide kept with the AUA AI Hub and based on the [AI Responsible Use Policy](policy.md) in force (effective July 30, 2025). The policy's own text governs, and these answers are not rulings of the AI Committee. Where an answer states something the policy requires, it says so; everything else is a suggestion with its reason. Your syllabus, the Student Handbook, the Employee Handbook, and clinical-site rules also apply.

## For everyone {: #for-everyone }

### What can I paste into an AI tool? {: #what-can-i-paste }

**Most everyday work:** your own notes and drafts, a rubric you wrote, a question you are working through. The policy prohibits putting sensitive, confidential, or proprietary information into publicly available AI tools, because those tools may keep, learn from, or pass on what you type. It names four kinds in particular, and its list is not exhaustive: protected health information (PHI); student education records protected by the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA), such as grades, evaluations, and identifiable student work; faculty and staff confidential personnel information; and AUA proprietary business information, unpublished research data, or trade secrets. The exception is a tool the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved for that data.

Taking the name off does not by itself make a case or a piece of work anonymous. What is left can still point to a person: an unusual condition or combination of findings, a date, a place, a role, or a detail that someone nearby would recognize. [Module 6: Clinical Contexts](../pathway/clinical.md#patient-information) shows how to turn a real case into a teaching abstraction, and [Module 3: The Policy in Practice](../pathway/rules.md) covers the rest in about ten minutes.

### Can I use a tool that isn't in the directory? {: #tool-not-listed }

**Yes, for ordinary work.** The policy has no restrictive approval list for tools used in general contexts, but it requires you to make sure any tool you use meets its data security, ethical, and legal requirements. Sensitive, confidential, or proprietary information is the exception: the policy keeps it out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data. [How Tools Are Reviewed](review-process.md) explains how to ask for a review.

### What AI tools does AUA provide or pay for? {: #what-aua-provides }

The Tool Directory's [list of what AUA licenses](../tools/index.md?task=licensed) shows the tools the directory records as licensed or procured by the university. Some other entries carry an Institutional cost label. That label means the tool needs some organization's license; it does not mean AUA holds one. A license is a procurement fact, not approval for patient or student records; under the policy, that approval is the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee's.

### What do Listed, Licensed, Draft, and Reviewed mean? {: #statuses }

In the Tool Directory, Listed means a tool is there because it is relevant and live; it is not a review, an endorsement, or an approval for sensitive data. Licensed means the university has licensed or procured the tool, a procurement fact rather than a review verdict. [How Tools Are Reviewed](review-process.md) defines every directory status. In the [Prompt Library](../prompts/index.md), Draft prompts are usable and still being tested and refined, and Reviewed prompts have completed that testing; both labels stay provisional until the AI Committee ratifies its review process.

## Studying and clinical training {: #studying-and-clinical-training }

### Can I use ChatGPT to study for block exams? {: #study-for-exams }

**Yes.** The policy encourages AI use where it helps you in your role, and it strongly discourages using an AI tool as your primary source of foundational knowledge: that source, it says, must remain the learning materials the school provides and the established academic literature. Three habits keep it useful:

- Build practice from your own lecture slides and notes, because your in-house exams are written from them.
- Leave National Board of Medical Examiners (NBME) and question-bank items out. They are licensed third-party content, and the policy does not allow licensed material into an AI tool without a review, because the license may not permit it.
- Check answers against your notes, because a model can be wrong in a confident voice. The policy requires you to verify AI-generated content before you use it in your academic work, and to check any reference an AI tool gives you at its original source.

To start: the [NBME-style question tutor](../prompts/index.md#nbme-style-question-tutor) or the [Flashcard builder](../prompts/index.md#flashcard-builder).

### Can I upload my lecture slides to study from? {: #lecture-slides }

**It depends on what the slides contain.** Building practice from your lecture slides is what this site's student guides suggest, and the policy does not single out course materials, but three of its provisions can apply to a particular deck:

- Slides can carry information the policy keeps out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved a tool for that data, such as a patient photo or case details in a clinical lecture.
- The policy requires that what you put into an AI tool does not infringe anyone's copyright.
- It does not allow material licensed from a third party, such as a publisher's figures or images from a licensed collection, into a tool without a review, because the license may not permit it.

The instructor who shared the deck knows where its material came from, so ask when you are unsure. What you make from the slides has a limit too: the policy requires AUA's prior written authorization to use or distribute AI-generated content that incorporates AUA's intellectual property outside the scope of your role, and it prohibits using AI to exploit AUA's intellectual property for personal monetary gain without prior written permission. Those limits can apply to a flashcard deck made from course slides, depending on whether it incorporates AUA's intellectual property and how you plan to use it.

### My syllabus says nothing about AI. What should I do? {: #syllabus-silent }

**For studying on your own, go ahead.** For anything you submit or are graded on, it is worth asking your instructor or course director first: your course sets what is authorized, and the policy requires you to acknowledge AI-generated content the way your course or department specifies. A short message usually settles it, and the Student Handbook treats unauthorized or unacknowledged AI use in coursework, assessments, or clinical training as possible academic misconduct.

### Can I practice differentials on patients I am seeing? {: #practice-differentials }

**Yes, once the case is a teaching abstraction:** an age band and a presentation pattern, with nothing left that could point back to the patient. Removing the name, dates, and places is where that starts, not where it ends; a rare condition, an unusual combination of findings, or a detail about someone's work or family can identify a patient, especially in a small community.

The policy prohibits putting patient health information into publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved a tool for that data, and a message, once sent, cannot be recalled. Your clinical site's own rules apply alongside the university's, so a use has to fit both. The [Clinical reasoning partner](../prompts/index.md#clinical-reasoning-partner) runs practice cases a step at a time; what changes when a real patient's care is involved is in [Module 6: Clinical Contexts](../pathway/clinical.md).

### Can I use AI on my residency application? {: #residency-application }

**Within what the organizations that handle your application allow.** The Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC), which runs the Electronic Residency Application Service (ERAS), accepts AI for brainstorming, proofreading, and editing a personal statement, as long as the final submission is your own work. The Educational Commission for Foreign Medical Graduates (ECFMG) advises against relying on AI to write it. Interview practice is the use the site's guide recommends most, because you can rehearse answers as often as you need. [AI and the Residency Application](../playbooks/residency-application.md) has the detail, with sources, including how to tell a patient story without identifying the patient; your Education Enhancement Department (EED) clinical advisor is the person to see about strategy.

## Teaching and assessment {: #teaching-and-assessment }

### Is it OK to use AI to give feedback on student essays? {: #essay-feedback }

**Yes, for formative feedback on work that no longer identifies the student.** Identifiable student work and grades are FERPA education records, and the policy prohibits putting them into publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved a tool for that data. Replacing names with Student A and Student B is a start, not the whole job: an essay can still identify its writer through a clinical encounter with its site and date, a personal story, or a detail classmates would recognize, so read for those before you paste.

The policy also requires you to work to identify and mitigate bias in anything AI contributes to an assessment, and to identify and attribute AI-generated content according to academic standards and your course or department's guidelines. Two suggestions keep it fair: keep the grade your own judgment, since you need to be able to explain it, and put the feedback in words you can stand behind. [Feedback on Student Writing](../playbooks/writing-feedback.md) walks through it.

### A detector flagged a student's work. What now? {: #detector-flag }

**Treat the flag as a reason to look more closely, not as proof.** Detectors produce false positives and false negatives, and they flag the writing of non-native English speakers more often, so a flag alone does not establish misconduct. The policy expects anyone who becomes aware of an actual or suspected violation to contact the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee ([Reporting Misuse](policy.md#reporting-misuse)), and the Student Handbook treats unauthorized or unacknowledged AI use as possible academic misconduct. The evidence on detectors is in [Common Misconceptions](../basics/misconceptions.md#ai-detectors-can-reliably-catch-ai-generated-writing); assessment design that makes this less likely is in [Module 4: Teaching and Assessment](../pathway/teaching-assessment.md).

### Can I use AI to draft a letter of recommendation? {: #letters-of-recommendation }

**Yes, as a drafting aid, and you remain the author.** The AAMC's guidance for letter writers says generative AI can support drafting and editing a letter, and that the writer remains its author, responsible for its content, accuracy, and the assessment it conveys. Grades, evaluations, and class rank are student education records, which the policy keeps out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved a tool for that data. A practical approach is to draft from your own account of what you observed, without the student's name or record details, and add those yourself in the final version. [For faculty writing letters](../playbooks/residency-application.md#for-faculty-writing-letters) quotes the AAMC.

## Research {: #research }

### Can I put my own unpublished manuscript into ChatGPT? {: #own-manuscript }

**Your own draft, yes: it is your work, and yours to share.** As a practical protection, work on it in a paid plan with training on your content turned off, so your text does not feed future versions of the model; that setting protects your work, and it is not what makes sharing your own draft acceptable.

Sections your co-authors wrote are theirs to share, so agree with them on the tools you will use. The data behind the work is different: the policy keeps unpublished research data and other sensitive or confidential information, research participant data among it, out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved a tool for that data, and an institutional review board (IRB) protocol, where one applies, sets its own limits. A manuscript you are peer reviewing is different again: it was shared with you in confidence, so under the policy it stays out of publicly available AI tools unless the Subcommittee has vetted and approved a tool for that data, and the journal's reviewer instructions may be stricter still.

### Where do I disclose AI use in a manuscript? {: #disclose-ai-use }

The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) recommendations put AI writing help in the acknowledgments and AI used in data collection or analysis in the methods, and an AI tool cannot be an author. Your target journal may add requirements, so read its instructions for authors early and note the tools you use as you go. [AI for Research](../tools/research.md#judged-by-the-same-rulers) covers the reporting standards.

**Example wording to adapt; your journal's instructions govern.**

Acknowledgments: "The authors used [tool and version] to [edit the manuscript for clarity and length]. The authors reviewed and revised all resulting text and take full responsibility for the content."

## Staff and administration {: #staff-and-administration }

### Can I paste a staff performance evaluation into ChatGPT? {: #staff-evaluation }

**Not into a public tool.** A performance evaluation is confidential personnel information, one of the four kinds the policy names, so it stays out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved a tool for that data. A directory status of Listed or Licensed does not change that. What works: ask for a structure or neutral phrasing that describes no one in particular, then add the specifics yourself, as [Administrative Drafting](../playbooks/admin-drafting.md) shows.

### Can I use AI for memos and meeting minutes? {: #memos-and-minutes }

**Yes.** Most administrative drafting is ordinary material for any capable assistant, including routine mentions of colleagues, such as who chairs a committee. The policy keeps sensitive, confidential, and proprietary information out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved a tool for that data; in administrative work that most often means confidential personnel information (an evaluation of someone's performance or conduct, for example), student education records, and AUA proprietary business information. For a document about one person's personnel or student matter, draft the structure with placeholders and add the specifics yourself.

One habit makes the costly error much less likely: give the model your facts as a list, ask it to mark anything missing rather than fill it in, and check every name, date, and commitment before you sign, because a model can invent or alter details. The policy requires you to identify and attribute AI-generated content according to academic standards and your course or department's guidelines, so follow your unit's practice. [Administrative Drafting](../playbooks/admin-drafting.md) walks through it.

## Who do I ask, and where do I report a problem? {: #who-to-ask }

The policy expects you to contact the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee immediately if you become aware of an actual or suspected violation of the policy; a breach of data privacy or security related to AI use; AI output that is erroneous, incomplete, misleading, offensive, harassing, discriminatory, or in violation of any university policy; or an AI system failure or significant malfunction. The [AI Committee page](committee.md#contact) gives the contact, which also takes questions. The policy commits the university to taking steps to protect the confidentiality of people who report, and it prohibits retaliation against anyone who reports in good faith. For anything about a particular course, ask the instructor or course director.
