# Can I Use AI for This?

<span class="meta-chip meta-status">Draft for AI Committee comment</span> <span class="meta-chip">For everyone</span>

Short answers to common questions about using artificial intelligence (AI) at the American University of Antigua (AUA), each with its conditions and a link to the detail. This is a plain-language guide kept with the AUA AI Hub and based on the [AI Responsible Use Policy](policy.md) in force (effective July 30, 2025; the policy provides for annual review). The policy's own text governs, and these answers are not rulings of the AI Committee. Where an answer states something the policy requires, it says so; the rest are suggestions, each with its reason, and guidance from other bodies, such as the Student Handbook, a journal, or the organizations that run residency applications, is named as theirs. The policy also requires you to follow all relevant university policies, ethical guidelines, codes of conduct, and applicable laws, and your syllabus or clinical site may set expectations of its own.

## For everyone {: #for-everyone }

### What does the policy ask of me every time? {: #every-time }

**The same requirements, whatever the task.** The policy covers students, faculty, and staff whenever they use AI tools for anything related to their role at AUA, wherever they are and whether the device is the university's or their own. In every such use, it asks the following:

- **Accountability.** You are ultimately accountable for your work, your decisions, and the final output, even when an AI tool helped.
- **Verification.** You must verify the accuracy and validity of AI-generated content before using it in academic work, research, or clinical application, and check any reference an AI tool gives you at its original source. The policy also expects you to know the limits of the tool you are using, to avoid over-reliance, and to evaluate its output critically.
- **Attribution.** You must identify and attribute any AI-generated content according to academic standards and specific course or departmental guidelines, and AI-generated content should not be presented as your own original work. Content counts as AI-generated if an AI tool produced any part of it, even if you edited it afterward.
- **Bias.** You must be aware that AI output can reflect biases in the data it was trained on, and actively work to identify and mitigate them so they do not affect educational assessments, administrative processes, or clinical decision-making.
- **Tool terms.** You must review and comply with the licensing agreements of the AI tools you use, and what a tool produces for you must not infringe copyright or other intellectual property rights.
- **Prohibited uses.** The policy prohibits using AI to misrepresent yourself or others (including generating fake documents or communications); to generate content that is illegal, harmful, or against AUA policies; to access, distribute, or disclose confidential or private information without authorization; and to adapt, reproduce, or exploit AUA intellectual property for personal monetary gain without prior written permission.

What you may put into a tool is the next answer.

### What can I paste into an AI tool? {: #what-can-i-paste }

**Anything that stays within the policy's limits on privacy and intellectual property:**

- **Sensitive, confidential, or proprietary information** stays out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data. The policy's reason is that such tools may collect, store, or monitor what you type, use it to train their models, or disclose it to others without the university's knowledge or control. It names four kinds in particular, and its list is not exhaustive: patient health information (PHI); student education records protected by the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA); faculty and staff confidential personnel information; and AUA proprietary business information, unpublished research data, or trade secrets.
- **In any AI tool**, public or not, your use must comply with privacy laws such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and FERPA, and with applicable data protection regulations.
- **Material that belongs to someone else**: what you put in must not infringe copyright or other intellectual property rights, and the policy prohibits putting data licensed from a third party into an AI tool without review, because doing so may violate the licensing agreement.

The policy does not set out a method for de-identifying information, and taking the name off does not by itself make a case or a piece of work anonymous: what is left can still point to a person, through an unusual condition or combination of findings, a date, a place, a role, or a detail that someone nearby would recognize. [Module 6: Clinical Contexts](../pathway/clinical.md#patient-information) shows how to turn a real case into a teaching abstraction, and [Module 3: The Policy in Practice](../pathway/rules.md) covers the policy in about ten minutes.

### Can I use a tool that isn't in the directory? {: #tool-not-listed }

**Yes, if the tool and your use meet the policy's requirements.** The policy keeps no restrictive approval list for tools used in general contexts, but it requires you to make sure any tool you use complies with its data security, ethical, and legal requirements. Sensitive, confidential, or proprietary information is the exception: the policy keeps it out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data. [How Tools Are Reviewed](review-process.md) explains how to ask for a review of a tool.

### What AI tools does AUA provide or pay for? {: #what-aua-provides }

The Tool Directory's [list of what AUA licenses](../tools/index.md?task=licensed) shows the tools the directory records as licensed or procured by the university. Some other entries carry an Institutional cost label, which means the tool needs some organization's license; the label does not say whether AUA holds one. A university license is a procurement fact, not the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee's specific approval, which is what the policy requires before sensitive, confidential, or proprietary information goes into a publicly available tool.

### What do Listed, Licensed, Draft, and Reviewed mean? {: #statuses }

In the Tool Directory, Listed means a tool is there because it is relevant and live; it is not a review, an endorsement, or an approval for sensitive data. Licensed means the university has licensed or procured the tool, a procurement fact rather than a review verdict. Reviewed means the tool has been examined through the process on [How Tools Are Reviewed](review-process.md), and its status note says which data categories, if any, the review cleared; that page defines all five directory statuses. In the [Prompt Library](../prompts/index.md), Draft prompts are usable and still being tested and refined, and Reviewed prompts have completed that testing; like every review status in the library, the label is provisional until the AI Committee ratifies it. A prompt's label describes testing of the prompt, not the tool or the data it is used with.

## Studying and clinical training {: #studying-and-clinical-training }

### Can I use ChatGPT to study for block exams? {: #study-for-exams }

**Yes, within the policy.** The policy encourages AI use where it helps you in your role, and it strongly discourages using an AI tool as your primary source of foundational knowledge: that source, it says, must remain the learning materials the school provides and the established academic literature. Three habits keep it useful:

- Build practice around the learning materials your course provides, because exams before your final term are written in-house from them. Before you upload slides, [check what they contain](#lecture-slides).
- Use National Board of Medical Examiners (NBME) and commercial question-bank items on their own platforms. They are licensed third-party content, and the policy prohibits putting data licensed from a third party into an AI tool without review, because doing so may violate the licensing agreement; practice built from your course materials avoids the problem.
- Check answers against your course materials and the literature, because a model can be wrong in a confident voice. The policy requires you to verify AI-generated content before you use it in your academic work, and to check any reference an AI tool gives you at its original source.

To start: the [NBME-style question tutor](../prompts/index.md#nbme-style-question-tutor) or the [Flashcard builder](../prompts/index.md#flashcard-builder).

### Can I upload my lecture slides to study from? {: #lecture-slides }

**It depends on what the slides contain, the tool, and what you do with the result.** Building practice from lecture slides is what this site's student guides suggest, and the policy does not single out course materials, but three of its provisions can apply to a particular deck:

- Slides can carry information the policy keeps out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data, such as identifiable patient information in a photo or case description in a clinical lecture.
- The policy requires that what you put into an AI tool, and what comes out, does not infringe copyright or other intellectual property rights.
- It prohibits putting data licensed from a third party, such as a publisher's figures or images from a licensed collection, into an AI tool without review, because doing so may violate the licensing agreement.

If you are unsure where slide material came from, the instructor who shared the deck is the person most likely to know, so ask. What you make from the slides has limits too: the policy requires prior written authorization from AUA to use or distribute, outside the scope of your role, AI-generated content that incorporates AUA's intellectual property, branding, or proprietary information, and it prohibits using AI to adapt, reproduce, or exploit AUA intellectual property for personal monetary gain without prior written permission. Those limits can apply to flashcards made from course slides, depending on what they incorporate and how you plan to use them.

### My syllabus says nothing about AI. What should I do? {: #syllabus-silent }

**For your own study, the policy is your guide. For anything you submit or are graded on, ask your instructor or course director first.** The reason to ask is the Student Handbook's AI section: "Unauthorized or unacknowledged use of AI in coursework, assessments, or clinical training may constitute academic misconduct." A syllabus that says nothing leaves open what is authorized. Ask how to acknowledge AI use, too: the policy requires you to identify and attribute any AI-generated content according to academic standards and specific course or departmental guidelines.

### Can I practice differentials on patients I am seeing? {: #practice-differentials }

**As practice, yes, if what you type into a public tool carries none of the patient's health information.** The policy prohibits putting patient health information into publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data, because such tools may keep, use, or pass on what you type without the university's knowledge or control. [Module 6: Clinical Contexts](../pathway/clinical.md#patient-information) suggests working from a teaching abstraction: an age band and a presentation pattern, with the identifying details gone. The policy does not say how far to strip a case, and removing the name, dates, and places is where that starts, not where it ends: a rare condition, an unusual combination of findings, or a detail about someone's work or family can identify a patient, especially in a small community.

Because these are real patients, the policy's requirements for clinical contexts apply as well. You must exercise extreme caution when using AI tools in clinical contexts. AI tools must not replace professional judgment or clinical expertise; they are meant to augment decision-making, not automate it. Any AI-assisted decision must comply with established clinical guidelines, ethical standards, and legal requirements, and the verification and bias requirements [above](#every-time) name clinical work explicitly. Module 6 suggests taking anything an AI suggestion raises about a real patient to your supervisor, whose judgment comes first. Hospitals and clinics also have their own AI policies, and on rotation a use has to fit the site's as well as the university's; your supervisor can tell you what the site allows. The [Clinical reasoning partner](../prompts/index.md#clinical-reasoning-partner) runs practice cases a step at a time.

### Can I use AI on my residency application? {: #residency-application }

**Yes, within the AUA policy and what the organizations that handle your application allow.** The Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC), which runs the Electronic Residency Application Service (ERAS), says AI tools are acceptable for brainstorming, proofreading, or editing a personal statement, and that the final submission should represent your own work. The Educational Commission for Foreign Medical Graduates (ECFMG) advises against relying on AI to write it. [AI and the Residency Application](../playbooks/residency-application.md) quotes both, with sources, and covers how to tell a patient story without identifying the patient. The AUA policy applies too, starting with your accountability for what you submit. Interview practice is the use that guide recommends most, because you can rehearse answers as often as you need, and your Education Enhancement Department (EED) clinical advisor is the person to see about strategy.

## Teaching and assessment {: #teaching-and-assessment }

### Is it OK to use AI to give feedback on student essays? {: #essay-feedback }

**Yes, if the use meets the policy's requirements.** The policy prohibits putting FERPA-protected student education records into publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data. It does not say when a piece of student work counts as such a record, so the practical approach [Feedback on Student Writing](../playbooks/writing-feedback.md) takes is to remove names and identifying details first, so that nothing the model sees points to a particular student. Replacing names with Student A and Student B is a start, not the whole job: an essay can still identify its writer through a clinical encounter with its site and date, a personal story, or a detail classmates would recognize, so read for those before you paste.

The policy makes you ultimately accountable for the grade and the feedback, even when AI helped, and it requires you to actively work to identify and mitigate bias so it does not affect educational assessments. It also requires you to identify and attribute AI-generated content according to academic standards and specific course or departmental guidelines; the playbook suggests disclosing your use the way your syllabus describes. The playbook covers formative feedback and leaves grades out, because models drift across a stack of essays and a grade is a judgment you need to be able to explain; it also describes how to pilot AI-assisted scoring by comparing it with your own marks.

### A detector flagged a student's work. What now? {: #detector-flag }

**Treat the flag as a reason to look more closely, not as proof.** Detectors can be wrong in both directions, flagging writing a person produced and missing writing a model produced, so a flag alone does not establish what happened. [Common Misconceptions](../basics/misconceptions.md#ai-detectors-can-reliably-catch-ai-generated-writing) explains their limits, and [Module 4: Teaching and Assessment](../pathway/teaching-assessment.md) covers how to treat a flag and the assessment design that makes this less likely. The policy does not say how to weigh a detector result. It does expect you to contact the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee immediately if you become aware of an actual or suspected violation of the policy ([Reporting Misuse](policy.md#reporting-misuse)), and the Student Handbook says unauthorized or unacknowledged use of AI in coursework, assessments, or clinical training may constitute academic misconduct.

### Can I use AI to draft a letter of recommendation? {: #letters-of-recommendation }

**Yes, within the policy, and you remain accountable for the letter.** The AAMC's guidance for letter writers, quoted in [For faculty writing letters](../playbooks/residency-application.md#for-faculty-writing-letters), says generative AI can support drafting and editing a letter, and that the writer remains its author, responsible for its content, accuracy, and the assessment it conveys. The policy keeps FERPA-protected student education records out of publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data, and the grades, evaluations, and class rank a letter draws on can be among them. A practical approach is to use the tool for structure and wording, working from your own account of what you observed without the student's name, record details, or anything else that identifies them, and to add the specifics yourself outside the tool.

## Research {: #research }

### Can I put my own unpublished manuscript into ChatGPT? {: #own-manuscript }

**It depends on what the manuscript contains; the policy makes no exception for your own work.** The policy prohibits putting sensitive, confidential, or proprietary information into publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data, and unpublished research data is one of the kinds it names. The restriction turns on what the text contains, not on who wrote it, and a draft manuscript usually reports results that are not yet published, so it can apply to the manuscript itself, not only to the data files behind it. The same clause covers other sensitive or confidential information in a study, such as data about research participants. If the study runs under an institutional review board (IRB) protocol, check what the protocol says about data handling, since it can set limits of its own.

Two things the policy does not settle: how far "unpublished research data" reaches into parts of a draft that report no results, such as a literature review or a methods section, and whether a paid plan with training on your content turned off counts as a publicly available tool. The [AI Committee contact](committee.md#contact) takes questions like these.

A practical point for whatever you do share: use a plan that lets you turn off training on your content, and turn it off, so that under the provider's own terms your text is not used to train its models. The setting does not by itself stop the provider from keeping your text for a time, terms vary by plan and change, and the policy requires you to review and comply with a tool's licensing agreements, so read them for training, retention, and sharing. A paid plan or a training setting is not the Subcommittee's specific approval, which is what the policy's exception requires.

Sections your co-authors wrote are partly their work, so agree with them on the AI tools you will use before you share a joint draft. A manuscript you are peer reviewing was shared with you in confidence: the policy keeps confidential information out of publicly available AI tools unless the Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data, and in any tool it prohibits using AI to disclose confidential information without authorization. Journals' reviewer instructions often address AI use, so read them before you use AI on a review.

### Where do I disclose AI use in a manuscript? {: #disclose-ai-use }

The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) recommendations put AI writing help in the acknowledgments and AI used in data collection or analysis in the methods, and an AI tool cannot be an author. The AUA policy separately requires you to identify and attribute AI-generated content, including text an AI tool produced that you then edited, according to academic standards and specific course or departmental guidelines. Your target journal may add requirements, so reading its instructions for authors early, and noting the tools you use as you go, saves piecing it together at submission. [AI for Research](../tools/research.md#judged-by-the-same-rulers) covers the reporting standards.

**Example wording to adapt to what you actually did and to your journal's instructions.**

Acknowledgments: "The authors used [tool and version] to [edit the manuscript for clarity and length]. The authors reviewed and revised all resulting text and take full responsibility for the content."

## Staff and administration {: #staff-and-administration }

### Can I paste a staff performance evaluation into ChatGPT? {: #staff-evaluation }

**Not into a publicly available tool, unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved it for that data.** A confidential performance evaluation falls in the category the policy calls faculty and staff confidential personnel information. A directory status of Listed or Licensed is not that approval, and in any tool the policy prohibits using AI to disclose confidential information without authorization. A practical approach, as [Administrative Drafting](../playbooks/admin-drafting.md) suggests: ask for a structure or neutral phrasing that describes no one in particular, then add the specifics yourself outside the tool, so the confidential details never enter the prompt.

### Can I use AI for memos and meeting minutes? {: #memos-and-minutes }

**Yes, within the policy; what needs care is what goes in.** Announcements, routine operational writing, and public facts such as who chairs a committee often carry none of the information the policy restricts. The policy prohibits putting sensitive, confidential, or proprietary information into publicly available AI tools unless the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee has specifically vetted and approved the tool for that data. Its examples include confidential personnel information, FERPA-protected student education records, and AUA proprietary business information, and minutes can carry any of them, so read a document for them before you paste it. For a document about one person's personnel or student matter, a practical approach is to draft the structure with placeholders, leaving out anything else that identifies the person, and add the specifics yourself outside the tool.

A practical accuracy check: give the model your facts as a list, ask it to mark anything missing rather than fill it in, and check every name, date, and commitment before you sign, because a model can invent or alter details and the document is yours to answer for. The policy requires you to identify and attribute any AI-generated content according to academic standards and specific course or departmental guidelines; if your department has not said how that applies to memos and minutes, ask it. [Administrative Drafting](../playbooks/admin-drafting.md) walks through it.

## Who do I ask, and where do I report a problem? {: #who-to-ask }

The policy expects you to contact the AI Responsible Use Subcommittee immediately if you become aware of an actual or suspected violation of the policy; a breach of data privacy or security related to AI use; AI output that is erroneous, incomplete, misleading, offensive, harassing, discriminatory, or in violation of any university policy; or an AI system failure or significant malfunction. The [AI Committee page](committee.md#contact) gives the contact for reports under the policy and for questions. The policy commits the university to taking steps to protect the confidentiality of people who report, and it prohibits retaliation against anyone who reports in good faith. Reports are investigated promptly, and the policy requires users to cooperate fully with an investigation and with any corrective measures that follow; the university may restrict a specific AI tool during or after an investigation. Misuse may lead to disciplinary action under AUA policies, and violations of privacy or intellectual property law may have legal consequences.

For questions short of a report: the policy says you should consult faculty members or other experts when you are unsure about the reliability or relevance of AI-generated content, and for what a particular course allows, the instructor or course director is the person to ask.
